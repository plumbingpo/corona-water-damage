How Long Drying Takes in a San Francisco Home

A common question from San Francisco customers is what to expect once the machines are running. On day one, the crew extracts the water, removes anything that cannot be dried, and sets air movers and dehumidifiers in the affected rooms. The equipment is loud, about like several large fans, and it makes the room warm. It needs to stay on around the clock, and turning it off, shifting it or airing out the room slows everything down.

Common Water Damage Issues in San Francisco

Water damage does not always arrive as a sudden gush across the kitchen. Below are the situations our crews are called to most often around San Francisco, and what each one tends to involve.

Hidden leaks: A pinhole in a supply line or a loose drain fitting can drip inside a wall for months. Watch for a water bill that climbs for no reason, soft spots on the floor, and paint that blisters. Thermal imaging and moisture readings let us map how far it spread before anything gets cut open. Even a slow leak matters, because wood and drywall that stay damp will rot and grow mold.

Sewage: A blocked sewer line sends waste back into the house, usually at the lowest shower, toilet or floor drain. It is typically caused by roots in the line, built-up grease or a municipal sewer overwhelmed during a downpour. Sewage counts as Category 3, the most contaminated class, and it is not safe to clean up bare-handed. Porous items it touches, such as carpet and pad, generally have to be removed.

Storms: Hard rain can outrun gutters and yard drains, then pool against the house. It enters at door thresholds, low windows and the garage, wherever the ground slopes toward the building. Because surface water carries dirt and chemicals from the ground it passed over, it is handled as unsanitary. A big enough storm can cause it at any time of year, and San Francisco properties are no exception.

Crawlspace: In homes built over a crawl space, few people look underneath, so moisture there builds up unseen. Standing water, a torn vapor barrier or a leaking pipe overhead keeps the air below your floors humid. You may notice cupped floorboards, a stale smell in the living area or insulation hanging loose. We pump out what has collected, dry the joists and subfloor, and show you where the moisture is coming from.

Condensate: Cooling equipment produces a steady stream of condensation whenever it runs in damp weather. It is meant to exit through a narrow drain tube, and that tube plugs up with slime and debris. When it backs up, the pan overflows into the ceiling, closet or hallway around the air handler. Most owners learn of it from a ceiling stain or drooping drywall, so have the drain line cleared once a year.
